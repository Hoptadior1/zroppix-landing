The short answer

There is no reliable evidence that clearing cookies lowers Amazon prices. The story comes from a 2000 incident, and the contemporary reports we could open do not mention cookies at all. What does move prices is better documented: who you are to Amazon (Prime or not), who the seller is, which colour or version you open, whether a coupon applies, and when you look.

Where the myth comes from

In September 2000, customers complained that Amazon charged different prices for the same DVDs. Computerworld (September 28, 2000) reported that the test covered 68 DVD titles over five days, with discounts of 20% to 40%. Amazon apologised and refunded about 6,896 customers an average of $3.10. Jeff Bezos said: "We've never tested and we never will test prices based on customer demographics," and "What we did was a random price test, and even that was a mistake."

ABC News (September 29, 2000) reported the same refund figures and Amazon's denial that the price differences were based on customer data. Neither article mentions cookies. The popular version, that deleting cookies got new-customer prices, is widely repeated, but we couldn't verify it from these sources, so we don't present it as fact.

Why the price history beats cookie tricks

Prices on Amazon change constantly, so a drop after clearing cookies is far more likely to be a coincidence than a reset.

  • Profitero (December 2013) said Amazon was making more than 2.5 million price changes a day. That is old, but it shows how fast prices can move.
  • A Joltekon jump starter was reported at $39.99 for Prime members and $71.99 for non-Prime on the same day (Hip2Save, March 20, 2026). That is a price gap with a known cause.
  • A Phomemo label maker was reported at $9.78 in August against a $35.99 list. A sale that big has nothing to do with your cookies.

What actually changes the price you see

  • Prime status. The Joltekon example above shows a $32 gap.
  • The seller. A third-party seller can price differently from Amazon.
  • The version. Colour, size and bundle can each carry a different price.
  • Coupons. A clippable coupon changes what you pay at checkout.
  • Time. Prices change during the day and around sales such as Black Friday on November 27 and Cyber Monday on November 30, 2026.

How to tell whether today's price is fair

We don't publish a price chart or a predicted price for the Amazon price checking on this page, because we can't back one up with recorded data here. What we can give you is a method that works for any product, and the free Zroppix extension that applies it for you on the Amazon page.

  1. Confirm the exact version. Model year, size, colour and bundle can each change the price. Read the title and the variant selector, not just the headline.
  2. Open the price history. Use the Zroppix extension (90-day history on Amazon pages) or another tracker such as CamelCamelCamel or Keepa. Where Amazon shows its own price history link, use that too.
  3. Compare today's price with the lowest in the window. At or near the low is a fair price. A big "percent off" badge on its own proves little, as our guide to sale badges explains.
  4. Check who sells it. Amazon or the brand is safest. Compare against the manufacturer's own site, which may run separate promotions.
  5. Set an alert if the price isn't right yet, and decide on your own timeline. Nobody can tell you what the price will do next.

Common mistakes

  • Clearing cookies and hoping. We found no evidence it works.
  • Believing a lower price proves a trick. Prices move for many reasons.
  • Ignoring the seller and the version. They change the price.
