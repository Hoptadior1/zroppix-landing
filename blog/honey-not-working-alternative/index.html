Why Honey Lost Millions of Users in 2026 — The Real, Documented Story

Honey's decline isn't a mystery or a rumor — it's one of the most thoroughly documented consumer-tech scandals in recent memory, with a viral investigation, 20+ lawsuits, and a major affiliate network formally cutting ties in January 2026. Here's what actually happened, sourced, plus a creator-safe alternative built in direct response.

⚡ Quick Answer

In December 2024, an independent investigation found that Honey (owned by PayPal) was replacing content creators' affiliate tracking with its own at checkout — redirecting commissions away from the creators who drove the sale. More than 20 lawsuits followed, and Rakuten Advertising formally dropped Honey from its affiliate network in January 2026. Honey had millions of users at its peak, promoted by over a thousand YouTube channels including some of the platform's biggest names. Its promise was simple: automatically find and apply coupon codes at checkout. What followed in December 2024 was one of the more significant consumer-tech controversies in recent memory — and it's worth understanding what actually happened, rather than just "Honey seems less useful lately."

The Documented Timeline

Dec 21, 2024

YouTuber MegaLag publishes "Exposing the Honey Influencer Scam," alleging Honey replaces creators' affiliate tracking cookies with its own at checkout via a practice known as last-click attribution hijacking. The video reaches millions of views within days.

Dec 2024

A Honey representative's email to MegaLag reportedly states: "If Honey is activated and is the last program used while shopping on a site, it is likely Honey will receive credit for the purchase" — effectively confirming the mechanism MegaLag described.

Jan 2025

YouTuber LegalEagle files a lawsuit against PayPal over the "sleeping leech" behavior. It's one of the first of what becomes a wave of litigation.

Through 2025

More than 20 class-action lawsuits are filed against PayPal, consolidated in part as In Re PayPal Honey Browser Extension Litigation, with plaintiffs including Wendover Productions and GamersNexus. A second MegaLag investigation later in 2025 adds further allegations, including that Honey's database listed roughly 181,000 stores while only about 35,000 had actual affiliate agreements with the platform.

Jan 12, 2026

Rakuten Advertising formally removes Honey from its affiliate network — a concrete, dated business consequence following roughly a year of user attrition reported in the millions.

📰 How the Mechanism Worked

Last-Click Attribution, Hijacked

Affiliate commissions are normally tracked through a "last-click attribution" cookie: when someone clicks a creator's affiliate link and later buys, the creator gets credit and commission. According to the investigation, when a shopper who'd arrived via a creator's link also activated Honey at checkout, Honey's own tracking would overwrite that cookie — becoming the "last click" and claiming the commission instead, even in cases where Honey found no discount at all.

PayPal disputed characterizations of the practice as fraudulent, but didn't deny that the mechanism itself — Honey activating and claiming last-click credit — occurred. The dispute has largely centered on framing and intent rather than whether the technical behavior happened.

Why Coupon Codes Were Never the Best Fit for Amazon

Separate from the scandal, Honey's core approach — searching for coupon codes — was always a better fit for smaller D2C retailers than for Amazon specifically:

  • Amazon coupon codes are relatively uncommon compared to boutique retail sites, which is why Honey often returned no results on Amazon searches.
  • When Amazon codes do exist, they typically apply to specific sellers, variants, or quantities — not the general listing.
  • A coupon code alone can't tell you if the underlying price is good. A 10% discount on an already-inflated price still leaves you overpaying.
