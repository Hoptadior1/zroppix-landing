How to Know If an Amazon Deal Is Actually Good (2026)

Amazon shows you a price crossed out in red. A percentage badge claims a big discount. A countdown timer says only hours remain. But whether that's actually a good deal is a separate question the page itself won't answer for you.

This isn't a hypothetical concern. A Real Lawsuit Confirmed This Concern Isn't Hypothetical

📋 Documented Case

Armstrong et al v. Amazon.com Inc. — a proposed class-action lawsuit filed September 22, 2025 in federal court in Washington state, on behalf of plaintiffs Cathy Armstrong and Oluwa Fosudo. The complaint alleges Amazon calculated its Prime Day 2025 percentage-off discounts using "fictional" list prices that hadn't actually been the product's price in the prior 90 days.

Specific examples cited in the complaint: a pair of headphones advertised at 44% off a listed $179.95 price, when the plaintiffs allege the item's real recent price range had been $130-160. Separately, a supplement product ("Neuriva Brain," 30 capsules) was advertised at 12% off a reference price of $44.99.

The case is documented by CBS News, ConsumerAffairs, FindLaw, and legal trackers including ClassAction.org, and remained an active, unresolved case as of coverage through mid-2026. It follows a separate, larger, already-resolved matter: Amazon's $2.5 billion settlement with the FTC (finalized September 25, 2025) over allegations it used dark patterns to enroll and retain Prime subscribers.

Whatever the eventual outcome of the Armstrong case, the underlying mechanics it describes — a "List Price" that doesn't reflect actual recent selling history — is exactly the pattern price-history tools are built to catch, regardless of what any individual retailer's discount badge claims.

🚨 The safe assumption, pending any resolution of ongoing litigation: don't take a percentage-off badge at face value during Prime Day, Black Friday, or any other sale event. Check the actual price history yourself before buying.

The 5-Step Amazon Deal Checker System

1

Check the recent price history

This is the single most important step. Install Zroppix free and open any Amazon product — you'll see the recent low, average, and high price. If the current "deal" price is above that recent average, it's not really a discount. If it's at or below, it's more likely genuine. CamelCamelCamel and Keepa also show price history charts if you prefer reading raw data yourself.

2

Don't anchor on the percentage-off badge

The "X% off" badge is only as meaningful as the reference price it's calculated from — and as the Armstrong case alleges, that reference price isn't always what it claims to be. Focus on the actual current price compared to the real recent price history, not the badge.

3

Compare against the recent average, not just the lowest-ever price

A price at the all-time low is obviously good, but that bar is often unrealistic. A more practical benchmark: is today's price at or below what the product has typically sold for over the last few months? That's a fair, achievable standard for "this is a reasonable time to buy."

4

Watch for a price spike right before a sale event

If the price history shows a jump in the weeks immediately before a sale event, and the "discount" price simply returns to what the product normally costs, that's the exact pattern described in the Armstrong lawsuit. Treat it with skepticism.

5

Compare across retailers

Amazon isn't always the cheapest option even during a sale event. A quick check of Best Buy, Walmart, or Target for the same product takes under a minute and sometimes turns up a better price with no membership required.

Common Amazon Deal Traps to Watch For

Lightning Deals

Lightning Deals use time pressure — a countdown timer and a "claimed" percentage — to encourage fast decisions. Some are genuinely good; others sit at or near the product's normal price. Check the price history before the timer affects your judgment, not after.

Coupon Clipping

Amazon sometimes shows an extra "clip this coupon" option on top of a listed price. Check the price history to see what the product actually cost before the coupon appeared — the base price is occasionally adjusted alongside the coupon offer.

Subscribe & Save

Subscribe & Save discounts can be smaller in practice than they first appear once you account for the fact that the same product often goes on sale periodically at a comparable or better price without a subscription commitment.
