Why use a price tracker at all?

An Amazon price is not a fixed number. Listings change price often, and a "deal" badge compares today's price with a reference price that may have little to do with what people actually paid. A price tracker keeps a record of what a product has really cost, so you can answer the only question that matters: is this price normal, good, or inflated?

That is useful for everything from a pair of headphones to a TV, and especially useful around big sales like Black Friday, when sale badges multiply. If you want the method in short form, it is to find the lowest price in the past year and compare it to today's.

1. Amazon's built-in price history

Amazon now shows price history on many product pages. According to Amazon's own announcement (May 1, 2026), the feature appears as a link next to the price, shows 30, 90 and 365 day views, and is available to customers in the U.S., UK, Canada and India. The 365-day view was still rolling out at that time, and Amazon says only eligible product pages show it. Shoppers can also ask Alexa for Shopping about a product's price history.

Good for: a free, zero-install first check. Limits: it's Amazon's own data on Amazon's own site, it isn't available everywhere or for every listing, and the announcement describes history, not a recommendation about whether to buy. If you only shop on Amazon occasionally, this may be all you need.

2. Keepa

Keepa is the data-heavy option. Its site says it provides Amazon price history charts and price drop alerts, offers price watches and browser extensions, and tracks more than 7 billion Amazon products. It supports several Amazon country stores, so check its current list if you shop outside the U.S.

Keepa also tracks things most shoppers never look at, such as sales rank and seller-related history, which is why it is popular with Amazon sellers and resellers. It offers paid plans for extra features; see keepa.com for what's included and current pricing. Good for: power users, sellers and anyone who likes detailed charts. Limits: there is a lot to take in, and the chart leaves the decision to you.

3. CamelCamelCamel

CamelCamelCamel is one of the best-known free options. You search for or paste a product to see a price chart, and you can set email alerts for price drops. It also offers a browser extension called The Camelizer, so you can see the chart without leaving the Amazon page. It's free and supported by advertising.

Good for: a simple, free chart and alerts. Limits: like Keepa, it gives you data rather than a verdict, and the site's look and depth are more basic than newer tools. See camelcamelcamel.com for the current features and supported Amazon stores.

4. PayPal Honey

Honey is a free browser extension best known for finding coupon codes. Its Amazon page says it also shows price history, lets you add items to a Droplist and notifies you when it detects a lower price, and compares other Amazon sellers, including estimated shipping.

Good for: people who want coupons, price alerts and seller comparison in one extension. Limits: price tracking is one feature among several, and, as with any extension that works across many stores, you should read what permissions you're granting. See Honey's Amazon page for details.

5. Zroppix (ours)

Zroppix is a Chrome and Brave extension that works on Amazon. Instead of leaving you with a chart, it reads a product's recent price history and gives a plain BUY or WAIT verdict, a savings estimate and a 90-day history right on the listing. The free plan includes email price alerts for up to three products. Paid plans add AI-based drop predictions, which you should treat as estimates, not promises.

Good for: shoppers who want a straight answer in the moment, not a chart to interpret. Limits, honestly: it currently works on Amazon only (the site says more stores are being added), its history window is 90 days, which is shorter than the 365 days Amazon now shows, and it can't give a useful verdict on a brand-new product with no history. It is also not built for sellers who need sales rank or Buy Box data.

How to choose

  • You shop on Amazon a few times a year: use Amazon's built-in history, and install a free extension for the big purchases.
  • You want a straight answer, not a chart: try Zroppix.
  • You love data or sell on Amazon: Keepa.
  • You want a simple, free chart and email alerts: CamelCamelCamel.
  • You already use Honey for coupons: its Droplist may be enough.
  • You are about to buy something expensive: check two sources. If Amazon's history and a third-party tool agree, you can be more confident.

There's no conflict in using more than one. They're free to run side by side, though too many extensions can slow your browser, so keep the ones you actually use.

What no price tracker can do

  • Predict the future with certainty. History shows what's normal. A price at its yearly low can still fall, and one above it can stay put.
  • Judge a product's quality. A low price on a poor product is still a poor buy.
  • Fix a bad variant or seller. Always check the model, size and who is selling.
  • Replace common sense. If you don't need it, it isn't a deal at any price.

A handy rule: use a tracker to avoid overpaying, not to chase the absolute bottom.

How to use any tracker well (60 seconds)

  1. Open the listing and the price history.
  2. Look at the longest window available (a year if possible).
  3. Note the lowest price and how often the product sits near it.
  4. Compare today's price. At or near the low is good. Well above it means wait or set an alert.
  5. Set an alert at the price you would be happy to pay.
  6. Before paying, confirm the model, the seller and the return window.

Using a tracker: upsides and limits

Why they help

  • A free option exists at every level
  • Amazon's built-in history needs no install
  • Several tools show the same history, so you can cross-check
  • Alerts mean you don't have to watch prices
  • Easy to combine two tools

Limits to know about

  • Free tiers often limit tracked products or checks
  • Extensions need browser permissions you should review
  • History can't predict the future
  • Coverage differs by country and by listing
  • Too many extensions can slow your browser

Pick-and-test checklist

Try this before committing to a tool.

  • Test it on a product you know: does the history match what you remember paying?
  • Check the window: can you see at least 90 days, ideally a year?
  • Check alerts: can you set one, and what are the free limits?
  • Read the permissions an extension asks for.
  • Check your country: does it support the Amazon store you use?
  • Cross-check one big purchase with two sources.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best Amazon price tracker?

It depends on how you shop. Amazon's built-in history is the easiest free start. Keepa is the deepest, CamelCamelCamel is a simple free option, Honey adds coupons, and Zroppix gives a plain BUY or WAIT verdict. Many people use Amazon's history plus one extension.

Does Amazon show price history itself now?

Yes, on eligible product pages in the U.S., UK, Canada and India, with 30, 90 and 365 day views according to Amazon's May 2026 announcement.

Is Keepa free?

Keepa offers free features and paid plans for more. See its site for what each includes and current pricing.

Is CamelCamelCamel free?

Yes. It offers free price charts and email alerts, and a browser extension called The Camelizer.

Is Zroppix free?

The core features are free: BUY or WAIT verdicts, a 90-day history and email alerts for up to three products. Paid plans add predictions and more. We make Zroppix, so check our site for current details.

Can a price tracker predict a price drop?

No. Trackers show history, and some give estimates, but nobody can know a future price. Treat predictions as guesses.

Do price trackers work on other stores?

Some do. Zroppix currently works on Amazon. Check each tool's supported sites.

Are price tracker extensions safe?

Review the permissions any extension requests, install from official stores, and keep only the ones you use.

Should I use more than one tracker?

You can. Cross-checking one big purchase with two sources is a good habit.

Pick one, test it, and stop overpaying

Start with Amazon's own history, add a tracker that suits how you shop, and always compare today's price with the yearly low.
