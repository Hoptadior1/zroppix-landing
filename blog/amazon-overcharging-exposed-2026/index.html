Are You Overpaying on Amazon? What's Actually Proven

A real federal investigation and a real 2025 lawsuit both confirm documented pricing practices in e-commerce — dynamic pricing based on personal data, and in Amazon's specific case, allegations of discount percentages calculated from list prices that didn't reflect actual recent selling history. Here's what's genuinely proven, and how to check any price yourself.

✍️ Zroppix Research Team ⏱ 10 min read 🔄 Updated July 17, 2026

It's a common suspicion: that the price you're shown on Amazon isn't necessarily fair, and that discount badges don't always mean what they claim. Rather than guessing at that, here's what's actually documented — a real federal study and a real, active lawsuit — and what it means for how you should shop.

📋 What's Actually Proven

Two real, independently verifiable sources back the core concern: the FTC's January 2025 surveillance pricing study confirmed retailers broadly use personal data to individualize prices, and a September 2025 lawsuit specifically alleges Amazon calculated Prime Day discounts from list prices that didn't reflect actual recent selling history. Neither source gives a precise percentage of "how often" this happens across all of Amazon — but both confirm the underlying mechanism is real, not just a shopper's suspicion.

Source 1 — The FTC's Surveillance Pricing Study

📋 Primary Source

FTC Surveillance Pricing Study — Staff Perspective, January 2025

Based on documents obtained through formal 6(b) orders sent to several companies, FTC staff found that consumer behaviors — from mouse movements on a webpage to which products a shopper leaves unpurchased in a cart — can be tracked and used to tailor pricing to individual consumers, based on characteristics like location, browsing patterns, and shopping history.

The study doesn't single out Amazon by name in its public findings, and it doesn't quantify a specific percentage of purchases affected industry-wide. What it establishes is that the underlying mechanism — using personal data to individualize prices for the same product — is real and documented at a federal regulatory level, not speculation.

Source 2 — A Real, Active Lawsuit Specifically About Amazon

📋 Documented Case

Armstrong et al v. Amazon.com Inc. — Filed September 22, 2025

A proposed class-action lawsuit filed in federal court in Washington state, on behalf of plaintiffs Cathy Armstrong and Oluwa Fosudo. The complaint alleges Amazon calculated its Prime Day 2025 percentage-off discounts using "fictional" list prices that hadn't actually been the product's price in the prior 90 days.

Specific examples cited in the complaint: a pair of headphones advertised at 44% off a listed $179.95 price, when the plaintiffs allege the item's real recent price range had been $130-160. A supplement product was separately advertised at 12% off a reference price the complaint alleges was similarly not representative of recent selling history.

The case is documented by CBS News, ConsumerAffairs, FindLaw, and legal trackers including ClassAction.org, and remained an active, unresolved case as of coverage through mid-2026.

It's worth being precise here: this is one specific lawsuit about one specific sale event, not a confirmed finding that applies to every Amazon product at every moment. But it's real, filed, and documented — a much stronger basis for concern than an anonymous "we found X%" claim with no verifiable methodology.

What This Actually Means for How You Shop

Neither source above tells you whether any specific product you're looking at right now is fairly priced. What they establish is that the pattern — a discount badge calculated from a reference price that doesn't reflect real recent selling history — is a documented, real phenomenon, not paranoia. That's exactly why checking a product's actual price history matters more than trusting the badge on the page.

What's Documented

Reference prices can be inflated relative to recent selling history

Confirmed specifically for Amazon by the Armstrong lawsuit's cited examples — a real, filed complaint with named plaintiffs and specific product allegations, not an anonymous claim.

What's Documented

Personalized pricing based on browsing behavior is a real, industry-wide practice

Confirmed by the FTC's federal study using subpoenaed documents from multiple companies — a regulatory-level finding, not a single retailer's marketing claim.

What's Not Established

A specific percentage of "how often" any given Amazon product is overpriced

Neither source provides this figure for Amazon specifically. Be skeptical of any claim citing a precise percentage (like "54% of products") without a transparent, verifiable methodology behind it.

Is Dynamic Pricing Legal?

Generally, yes, in the US and most of the EU, as long as pricing doesn't target legally protected characteristics like race, gender, or religion. Behavioral and algorithmic pricing based on browsing and purchase signals is currently permitted — which is exactly why independent price-history tools matter: they restore transparency that the pricing system itself doesn't provide.

Whatever the eventual outcome of the Armstrong lawsuit, the practical habit — checking real price history before trusting a discount badge — protects you regardless of how any individual legal case resolves.
