Important Update: The M4 Was Discontinued

Apple replaced the MacBook Air M4 with the M5 in March 2026, and as of mid-2026 the M4 is no longer sold new through Apple directly — remaining units are available through Amazon, Apple Certified Refurbished, and other authorized retailers clearing inventory, typically at $750-950 depending on configuration. The M5 starts at $1,299 (13-inch), a real price increase from the M4's original $999 launch price. For most students, the chassis, display, ports, and 18-hour battery life are identical between the two — the M5's main advantages (roughly 50% faster GPU, Wi-Fi 7) matter mainly for sustained video editing or 3D work, not typical coursework. That makes the discontinued M4 a genuinely strong value pick while clearance stock remains available.

ℹ️

If M4 stock runs out: The M5 MacBook Air becomes the direct equivalent recommendation — same core experience, faster chip, higher price. Everything in this guide about who should buy "the Air" applies to either chip generation; the M4 is simply the better value while it's still available.

Four Devices, Four Different Student Budgets

This guide covers four genuinely different price and use-case tiers: the discounted MacBook Air M4 (best value while stock lasts) or the current M5 (if M4 sells out) for students who need a full laptop, the MacBook Neo for students on a tighter budget whose work is mostly browser-based, and the iPad Air M4 for students who prioritize portability and mainly take notes, read, and consume content rather than run desktop software.

Who Should Buy the MacBook Air (M4 or M5)

For the majority of students — including STEM majors running coding environments, business students building spreadsheets and presentations, and writing-heavy majors managing long research papers with citation managers — a MacBook Air is the right default. Both the clearance M4 and current M5 share 16GB base RAM (avoiding the multitasking bottleneck smaller devices hit), full access to desktop-grade software, and an 18-hour battery that realistically gets through a full day of back-to-back classes without a charger. Given the two chips deliver a nearly identical everyday experience, the M4 at clearance pricing is the smarter buy unless you specifically need the M5's GPU or Wi-Fi 7 improvements.

Who Should Buy the MacBook Neo Instead

The MacBook Neo exists specifically for students where budget is the binding constraint and the workload is genuinely light — web browsing, Google Docs, Zoom classes, email, and video streaming. Its A18 Pro chip is an iPhone-class processor, not a full Mac-class chip, and the 8GB of non-upgradeable RAM will start to feel constrained if you regularly run more than a handful of demanding apps at once. For a student whose entire workload fits that lighter profile, the Neo delivers real macOS at a meaningfully lower price than either Air — but anyone anticipating coding, video editing, or heavy multitasking should budget for an Air instead rather than upgrading later, since the RAM cannot be added after purchase.

If there's any chance your major will require running a coding IDE, virtual machine, or design software alongside a browser with many tabs, the extra cost for 16GB now is cheaper than replacing an 8GB device a year in.

Who Should Buy the iPad Air M4 Instead

The iPad Air M4 makes the most sense for students whose primary needs are note-taking, reading textbooks and PDFs, and light writing — particularly with Apple Pencil support for handwritten notes and annotation. Its 12GB of unified RAM and portability (under 1.1 lbs for the 11-inch model) are real advantages for students who carry their device between multiple classes across campus all day. The trade-off is real: iPadOS cannot run most desktop-only software, so any coursework requiring a specific Windows or macOS application, a full coding IDE, or complex spreadsheet work will hit a wall. Students considering an iPad-first approach should honestly assess their major's software requirements before committing to it as their only device.

Should You Buy Both a Laptop and a Tablet?

Some students genuinely benefit from pairing a laptop with a smaller, cheaper tablet purely for note-taking and reading, rather than trying to make one device do everything. If budget allows, a MacBook Air for coursework paired with a base iPad (not the Air) for lecture notes covers both use cases without overspending on a single do-everything device. For students on a strict single-device budget, the comparison above should guide which trade-off — full software compatibility versus portability — matters more for your specific major and daily routine.

Timing — Why July Is the Right Window

July sits in a genuinely useful gap in the yearly discount calendar: Prime Day discounting in June has already happened, and the heaviest back-to-school demand — which tends to push prices back up rather than down — doesn't peak until August. Buying now, in mid-to-late July, gives you a real buffer before fall semester starts to handle shipping, data migration, and any early troubleshooting, without competing against the August rush. Amazon's own Back to School promotional period typically overlaps with this window as well.

What to Do Before Your First Day of Class

Whichever device you land on, a few setup steps make the first week of the semester noticeably smoother. Set up cloud backup — iCloud, Google Drive, or OneDrive — before you need it, not after a laptop is lost or a file is corrupted mid-semester. Install your school's required software early, since campus IT support lines are typically far less backed up in July than during the first week of classes. Finally, if you're moving from Windows to macOS or from a laptop-primary workflow to an iPad-primary one, budget a genuine week or two of adjustment time to relearn keyboard shortcuts, file management, and app equivalents — that adjustment period is far less stressful in July than once graded coursework is already due.

Frequently Asked Questions
What laptop should I buy for college in 2026?+
For most college students, a MacBook Air (M5 new, or M4 while clearance stock lasts) is the best overall choice — enough performance for coursework, coding, and creative work, full macOS, all-day battery life. The M4 was discontinued in March 2026 in favor of the M5, so remaining M4 stock is now sold at clearance pricing, often $300+ below the M5's starting price, while supplies last. Students on a tighter budget who mainly need a browser and word processor should consider the MacBook Neo instead.
Is an iPad or a laptop better for college students?+
It depends on your major and workflow. An iPad works well for note-taking, reading, and light writing. A laptop is necessary for coding, running desktop-only software, multitasking across many windows, or coursework requiring specific applications not available on iPadOS. Many STEM, business, or writing-heavy students need a full laptop; students in note-taking-heavy programs can sometimes get by with an iPad alone.
Is 8GB RAM enough for a college laptop in 2026?+
For standard coursework — browsing, word processing, email, video calls, light photo editing — 8GB is generally sufficient on Apple Silicon Macs due to efficient unified memory. Students in computer science, video editing, 3D modeling, or data science running multiple demanding applications should consider 16GB instead.
When is the best time to buy a college laptop?+
July is a strong window — it falls after Prime Day discounting and before the price-sensitive back-to-school rush in August pushes demand up. Buying in July or early August also leaves enough time to set up the device before fall semester starts. The next major discount window after back-to-school is Black Friday in November, too late for day-one needs.
